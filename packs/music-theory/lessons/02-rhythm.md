## Why rhythm matters as much as pitch

Pitch tells you *which* note; rhythm tells you *when* and *for how long*. A melody played with the right pitches but the wrong rhythm is often unrecognisable. This lesson gives you the symbols for length, the rests that measure silence, and the time signature that groups beats into bars.

## The beat

Most music has a steady pulse you can tap your foot to: the **beat**. Speed is measured in beats per minute (BPM); 60 BPM is one beat per second. Note lengths are written relative to the beat, so the same written rhythm can be played fast or slow.

## Note values

Each note value is half the length of the one before it:

| Name (UK name) | Length in quarter-note beats |
|---|---|
| Whole note (semibreve) | 4 |
| Half note (minim) | 2 |
| Quarter note (crotchet) | 1 |
| Eighth note (quaver) | 1/2 |
| Sixteenth note (semiquaver) | 1/4 |

A whole note is an open oval. A half note adds a stem. A quarter note fills the head in. Each **flag** on the stem halves the length again: one flag for an eighth, two for a sixteenth. Groups of eighths and sixteenths are usually joined by **beams** instead of flags, which makes beats easier to see.

## Rests

Every note value has a matching **rest**, a symbol for silence of the same length. A whole rest hangs below a line; a half rest sits on top of a line (a "hat"). A whole rest is also used for "a whole bar of silence" in any time signature.

## Dots and ties

A **dot** after a note adds half of that note's own value. A dotted half note is 2 + 1 = 3 beats. A dotted quarter is 1 + 1/2 = 1.5 beats. A dotted eighth is 1/2 + 1/4 = 3/4 of a beat.

A **tie** is a curved line joining two notes of the **same pitch**. You play the first and hold it through the second, so the lengths add up. Ties are needed when a note lasts across a bar line, which a dot cannot do. (A similar curve over notes of *different* pitches is a **slur**, which means "play smoothly", not "add the lengths".)

## Bars and time signatures

Music is divided into **bars** (measures) by vertical **bar lines**. The **time signature** at the start, written as two stacked numbers, says how each bar is built:

- The **top number** says how many beats are in a bar.
- The **bottom number** says which note value gets one beat: 4 means a quarter note, 2 a half note, 8 an eighth note.

So **4/4** has four quarter-note beats per bar (it is so common it is called "common time" and sometimes written C). **3/4** has three quarter-note beats, the waltz feel: ONE two three. **2/4** has two, like a march.

### Simple and compound time

In 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4 each beat splits naturally into **two** halves. These are **simple** time signatures.

In **6/8** the bar holds six eighth notes, but they are felt as two groups of three: ONE two three FOUR five six. So 6/8 has **two** beats per bar, and each beat is a **dotted quarter** that splits into three. Time signatures whose beats split into three are **compound**. 6/8 is compound duple (two beats), 9/8 compound triple (three beats), 12/8 compound quadruple (four beats).

## Counting a bar

Musicians count beats with numbers and their halves with "and": in 4/4, eighth notes are counted "1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and". Checking a bar means adding its values: in 3/4 a half note plus a quarter note fills the bar (2 + 1 = 3), and so does a dotted half note.

## Common mistakes

> **Adding the dot's own length instead of half.** A dot adds half of the note it follows, not a fixed half beat. A dotted half note is 3 beats, not 2.5.

> **Reading 6/8 as six beats.** At normal speed 6/8 has two beats, each a dotted quarter.

> **Confusing ties with slurs.** A tie joins two notes of the same pitch into one longer note. A slur covers different pitches and only changes how they are played.

## Summary

Each note value halves the previous one: whole 4, half 2, quarter 1, eighth 1/2, sixteenth 1/4 (in quarter-note beats). Rests are silences of the same lengths. A dot adds half the note's value; a tie adds two notes of the same pitch. The time signature's top number gives beats per bar and the bottom number the beat's note value. 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4 are simple; 6/8 is compound with two dotted-quarter beats.

```solution
In simple time each beat divides naturally into two equal parts; in compound time each beat divides into three. 3/4 has three quarter-note beats, and each quarter splits into two eighths, so the six eighths are grouped 2+2+2 and counted "1 and 2 and 3 and". 6/8 has two beats, and each beat is a dotted quarter that splits into three eighths, so the same six eighths are grouped 3+3 and counted "ONE two three FOUR five six".
```
