## Why intervals

An **interval** is the distance between two pitches. Melodies are chains of intervals; chords are stacks of them. Naming intervals precisely lets you describe why a chord sounds bright or dark, why a melody leaps or steps, and how to transpose anything into another key.

Every interval name has two parts: a **number** (second, third, fourth…) and a **quality** (major, minor, perfect, augmented, diminished). "Major third" and "perfect fifth" are typical names.

## Step 1: the number

Count **letter names**, including both ends. C up to E is C-D-E: three letters, a **third**. C up to G is C-D-E-F-G: a **fifth**. C up to the next C is an **octave** (an eighth). The same two letters at the same pitch is a **unison**.

The number ignores sharps and flats: C to E, C to E♭ and C♯ to E♭ are all thirds. The accidentals only change the quality.

## Step 2: the quality

Measure the interval against the major scale of the **lower** note:

- Unisons, fourths, fifths and octaves that land on the major scale are **perfect** (P).
- Seconds, thirds, sixths and sevenths that land on the major scale are **major** (M).
- A major interval made a half step smaller is **minor** (m).
- A perfect or major interval made a half step larger is **augmented** (A).
- A perfect or minor interval made a half step smaller is **diminished** (d).

So C to E is a major third (E is in C major), C to E♭ is a minor third, and C to G♭ is a diminished fifth.

## Half-step counts

Counting half steps is a reliable check:

| Half steps | Interval | Example from C |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Perfect unison (P1) | C–C |
| 1 | Minor second (m2) | C–D♭ |
| 2 | Major second (M2) | C–D |
| 3 | Minor third (m3) | C–E♭ |
| 4 | Major third (M3) | C–E |
| 5 | Perfect fourth (P4) | C–F |
| 6 | Augmented fourth / diminished fifth (the **tritone**) | C–F♯ / C–G♭ |
| 7 | Perfect fifth (P5) | C–G |
| 8 | Minor sixth (m6) | C–A♭ |
| 9 | Major sixth (M6) | C–A |
| 10 | Minor seventh (m7) | C–B♭ |
| 11 | Major seventh (M7) | C–B |
| 12 | Perfect octave (P8) | C–C |

Two intervals with the same half-step count but different letter spans are enharmonic: C–F♯ (augmented fourth) and C–G♭ (diminished fifth) are both 6 half steps. Which name is right depends on the letters, so always count letters first, then half steps.

## Worked examples

**E up to G.** Letters E-F-G: a third. E major contains G♯, so E–G♯ is a major third; G is a half step lower, so **E–G is a minor third** (3 half steps: E–F, F–F♯, F♯–G).

**D up to A♭.** Letters D-E-F-G-A: a fifth. D major contains A, so D–A is a perfect fifth; A♭ is a half step smaller, so **D–A♭ is a diminished fifth** (6 half steps, a tritone).

**B♭ up to F.** Letters B-C-D-E-F: a fifth. B♭ major contains F, so **B♭–F is a perfect fifth**.

## Consonance and dissonance

Some intervals sound stable (**consonant**): unisons, octaves, perfect fifths and fourths, and major and minor thirds and sixths. Others sound tense and want to move (**dissonant**): seconds, sevenths and the tritone. Dissonance is not "wrong"; it creates the tension that makes resolution satisfying, as you will hear in the cadences lesson.

## Inversion

Turning an interval upside down (moving the lower note up an octave) **inverts** it. The numbers add to 9 and the qualities swap: major ↔ minor, augmented ↔ diminished, perfect stays perfect. C–E is a major third; E–C is a minor sixth (3 + 6 = 9).

## Common mistakes

> **Counting from zero.** C to E is a third because you count C as 1. Counting "steps between" gives 2, which is wrong.

> **Naming by half steps alone.** 6 half steps can be an augmented fourth or a diminished fifth. The letters decide.

> **Using the upper note's scale.** Quality is measured against the major scale of the lower note.

## Summary

Interval number = letters spanned, counting both ends. Quality = compare with the lower note's major scale: perfect for 1, 4, 5, 8; major for 2, 3, 6, 7; a half step smaller than major is minor; larger is augmented; smaller than perfect or minor is diminished. Half steps: m2 1, M2 2, m3 3, M3 4, P4 5, tritone 6, P5 7, m6 8, M6 9, m7 10, M7 11, P8 12. Inversions add to 9 and swap major/minor.
