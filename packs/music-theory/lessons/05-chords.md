## Why chords

A **chord** is three or more notes sounding together. Most songs can be described as a melody over a sequence of chords, and the chord symbols on a lead sheet (C, Am, G7) are the shorthand guitarists and pianists read every day. This lesson builds the basic chord, the **triad**, and then the seven triads that belong to a major key.

## Building a triad

A **triad** is three notes stacked in thirds: a **root**, the **third** above it and the **fifth** above it. On the staff a triad in root position looks like a snowman: line-line-line or space-space-space.

The C major triad is C–E–G: root C, a major third up to E, a minor third up to G (which is a perfect fifth above C).

## Four qualities

The two thirds can each be major or minor, giving four triad qualities:

| Quality | Lower third | Upper third | Root to fifth | Example | Symbol |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Major | M3 | m3 | P5 | C–E–G | C |
| Minor | m3 | M3 | P5 | C–E♭–G | Cm |
| Diminished | m3 | m3 | d5 | C–E♭–G♭ | C° (Cdim) |
| Augmented | M3 | M3 | A5 | C–E–G♯ | C+ (Caug) |

Major triads sound bright and stable, minor triads darker but still stable, diminished triads tense, augmented triads unsettled. Major and minor triads have a perfect fifth; that shared fifth is why both feel at rest.

## The chords of a major key

Build a triad on each degree of the major scale, using only notes from the scale. In C major (all white keys):

| Degree | Triad | Quality | Roman numeral |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | C–E–G | major | I |
| 2 | D–F–A | minor | ii |
| 3 | E–G–B | minor | iii |
| 4 | F–A–C | major | IV |
| 5 | G–B–D | major | V |
| 6 | A–C–E | minor | vi |
| 7 | B–D–F | diminished | vii° |

The pattern **I ii iii IV V vi vii°** (major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished) is the same in every major key, because every major scale has the same step pattern. **Roman numerals** name chords by scale degree rather than letter: upper case for major, lower case for minor, a small circle for diminished. That way "I–IV–V" describes the same progression in any key: C–F–G in C major, G–C–D in G major, D–G–A in D major.

Three chords do most of the work: **I** (tonic, home), **IV** (subdominant, moving away) and **V** (dominant, tension that wants to return home). Many folk, rock and blues songs use only these three.

## Inversions

A triad does not have to have its root at the bottom. With the **third** in the bass it is in **first inversion** (C/E: E–G–C); with the **fifth** in the bass it is in **second inversion** (C/G: G–C–E). The chord's name and quality do not change; inversions make bass lines smoother. The slash symbol C/E means "C chord, E in the bass".

## Seventh chords in one paragraph

Adding another third on top of a triad gives a **seventh chord**. The most important is the **dominant seventh**, built on degree 5: in C major G–B–D–F, written **G7** or **V7**. It is a major triad plus a minor seventh, and the tritone between B and F gives it a strong pull towards the tonic chord.

## Common mistakes

> **Taking the third from the wrong scale.** For a D minor triad, start from D major's third (F♯) and lower it: D–F–A. Don't guess.

> **Thinking vii° is minor.** In a major key the triad on degree 7 has a diminished fifth (B–F in C major), so it is diminished.

> **Changing a chord's name when it is inverted.** E–G–C is still a C major chord, in first inversion.

## Summary

A triad is root, third and fifth stacked in thirds. Major = M3 + m3, minor = m3 + M3, diminished = m3 + m3, augmented = M3 + M3. In a major key the triads are I ii iii IV V vi vii°. Roman numerals describe progressions in any key; I, IV and V are the core. Inversions put the third or fifth in the bass without changing the chord. V7 is a major triad plus a minor seventh.

@write
From memory, explain why the Roman numeral pattern I ii iii IV V vi vii° is the same in every major key, and give the I, IV and V chords of D major.
min: 50
```solution
Every major scale is built with the same pattern of whole and half steps, W W H W W W H. A triad built on a given scale degree uses the scale's own notes, so the sizes of its thirds depend only on where the half steps fall relative to that degree, and those positions are identical in every major key. So the chord on degree 1 is always major, degree 2 always minor, and so on, giving I ii iii IV V vi vii° everywhere. D major is D E F sharp G A B C sharp, so I is D major (D F# A), IV is G major (G B D) and V is A major (A C# E).
```
! Check you tied the pattern to the fixed step pattern and that your D major chords are D, G and A.

--- recall
Q: Interval make-up of major, minor, diminished and augmented triads?
A: Major M3+m3; minor m3+M3; diminished m3+m3; augmented M3+M3.
Q: Roman numerals of the triads in a major key?
A: I ii iii IV V vi vii°.
Q: What is first inversion?
A: The triad's third is in the bass (e.g. C/E).
Q: What notes make G7, and what makes it pull to C?
A: G–B–D–F; the tritone B–F resolves to C–E.
