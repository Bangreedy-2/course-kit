## Why progressions

Chords on their own are colours; a **progression** is a sentence. Some chords sound like questions and some like answers. Understanding why lets you hear the structure of a song, write your own and predict where a piece is going. This lesson covers the "gravity" of the tonic, the most common progressions, and the four **cadences** that end musical phrases.

## Chord functions

The chords of a key fall into three families by what they do:

- **Tonic (I, also vi and iii):** stable, home.
- **Predominant (IV and ii):** moving away from home, leading towards the dominant.
- **Dominant (V, V7 and vii°):** tension that wants to resolve to the tonic.

A very common shape for a phrase is **tonic → predominant → dominant → tonic**. In C major, C–F–G–C (I–IV–V–I) or C–Dm–G7–C (I–ii–V7–I) are textbook examples.

Why does V pull to I? V contains the **leading tone** (degree 7, B in C major), a half step below the tonic, which wants to rise to it. V7 adds degree 4 (F), a half step above degree 3 (E), which wants to fall. Those two half-step resolutions are the strongest "pull" in tonal music.

## Common progressions

| Progression | In C major | Where you hear it |
|---|---|---|
| I–IV–V–I | C–F–G–C | Folk, rock, hymns |
| I–V–vi–IV | C–G–Am–F | Countless pop songs |
| ii–V–I | Dm–G–C | Jazz standards |
| I–vi–IV–V | C–Am–F–G | 1950s doo-wop |
| 12-bar blues | I I I I · IV IV I I · V IV I V | Blues, early rock and roll |

These are written in Roman numerals so you can move them to any key: I–V–vi–IV in G major is G–D–Em–C.

## Cadences

A **cadence** is the pair of chords at the end of a phrase. It works like punctuation:

| Cadence | Chords | Effect | Like |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Authentic** | V → I | Strong, final | a full stop |
| **Plagal** | IV → I | Softer close, the "Amen" ending of hymns | a gentle full stop |
| **Half** | any chord → V | Open, unfinished, a pause | a comma or question mark |
| **Deceptive** | V → vi | Expected I, got vi instead: surprise | a plot twist |

An authentic cadence is **perfect** when both chords are in root position and the melody ends on the tonic; it is the most final-sounding ending in music. A half cadence often closes the first half of a melody, and the second half answers it with an authentic cadence: a question and its answer.

## Hearing it

You can practise without an instrument. Sing or hum the tonic of a song you know (the note that sounds like "home" at the end). Then listen to the last two chords of each line. If the line sounds finished, it is likely an authentic or plagal cadence; if it sounds like it is waiting, it is likely a half cadence. When a song seems about to end but swerves to a sad-sounding chord instead, that is often a deceptive cadence.

## Common mistakes

> **Calling any ending on I "authentic".** Only V → I is authentic. IV → I is plagal.

> **Thinking a half cadence goes from V.** A half cadence ends **on** V; the chord before it can be anything (often I, ii or IV).

> **Treating progressions as fixed letter names.** Learn them in Roman numerals; the letters depend on the key.

## Summary

Chords have functions: tonic (I, vi, iii), predominant (IV, ii), dominant (V, V7, vii°); phrases often go T → PD → D → T. V pulls to I because the leading tone rises a half step to the tonic. Common progressions: I–IV–V–I, I–V–vi–IV, ii–V–I, I–vi–IV–V and the 12-bar blues. Cadences: authentic V–I, plagal IV–I, half ending on V, deceptive V–vi.

min: 50
```solution
V contains the leading tone, scale degree 7, which in C major is B. It lies a half step below the tonic C and tends to rise to it. A V7 chord, G B D F, also contains scale degree 4, F, which is a half step above degree 3, E, and tends to fall to it. When G7 moves to C major (C E G), B rises to C and F falls to E: two half-step resolutions happen at once. B and F also form a tritone, a dissonance, so the move resolves tension into the stable C major triad. That is why the authentic cadence V to I sounds so final.
```
