## Why this comes first

Every other idea in this course (scales, intervals, chords) is a way of talking about **pitch**, how high or low a sound is. Before you can talk about the space between two notes, you need names for the notes and a way to write them down. This lesson gives you both: the seven letter names, the two symbols that move a note up or down, and the five-line staff with its two common clefs.

## Seven letters, repeating

Western music names notes with the first seven letters of the alphabet: **A B C D E F G**. After G the names start again at A, one step higher. The distance from one note to the next note with the same name (C up to the next C) is an **octave**. Two notes an octave apart sound like "the same note, higher", which is why they share a name.

On a piano the white keys carry these seven names. Find the groups of two black keys: the white key just to the left of each group is **C**. From there the white keys read C D E F G A B, then C again.

To say *which* C you mean, musicians add an octave number. **Middle C**, the C nearest the middle of the piano, is **C4**. The B just below it is B3, and the D just above it is D4: the number changes at every C, not at every A. The A above middle C, **A4**, is the usual tuning note and vibrates 440 times a second (440 Hz).

## Half steps, whole steps, sharps and flats

The smallest distance between two notes in Western music is a **half step** (also called a semitone). On a piano it is the distance from any key to the very next key, black or white. Two half steps make a **whole step** (a tone).

Look at the keyboard again. Most neighbouring white keys have a black key between them, so they are a whole step apart. Two pairs do not: **E–F** and **B–C** are only a half step apart. Remember those two pairs; they explain the shape of every scale in the next lessons.

The black keys get their names from their white neighbours:

- A **sharp** (♯, often typed `#`) raises a note by a half step. The black key just right of C is **C♯**.
- A **flat** (♭, often typed `b`) lowers a note by a half step. That same black key is also **D♭**.
- A **natural** (♮) cancels a sharp or flat.

Two names for the same key, like C♯ and D♭, are called **enharmonic** equivalents. Which name is correct depends on the key you are in, as you will see in the scales lesson. Sharps and flats can also apply to white keys: E♯ is the same key as F, and C♭ is the same key as B.

## The staff

Music is written on a **staff** (or stave) of five horizontal lines. Each line and each space between lines stands for one letter name; moving up one position (line to space, or space to line) moves up one letter. Notes higher or lower than the staff sit on short extra lines called **ledger lines**.

A **clef** at the start of the staff fixes which line is which note.

### Treble clef

The **treble clef** (𝄞, also called the G clef) curls around the second line from the bottom, marking it as **G4**, the G above middle C. Reading upwards:

| Position | Note |
|---|---|
| Lines, bottom to top | E G B D F |
| Spaces, bottom to top | F A C E |

The classic memory aids: lines are "**E**very **G**ood **B**oy **D**eserves **F**ruit", spaces spell **FACE**. Middle C sits on one ledger line just below the treble staff.

### Bass clef

The **bass clef** (𝄢, also called the F clef) puts its two dots around the second line from the top, marking it as **F3**, the F below middle C.

| Position | Note |
|---|---|
| Lines, bottom to top | G B D F A |
| Spaces, bottom to top | A C E G |

Memory aids: "**G**ood **B**oys **D**o **F**ine **A**lways" and "**A**ll **C**ows **E**at **G**rass". Middle C sits on one ledger line just above the bass staff, so the two staves of piano music meet at middle C.

## Accidentals on the staff

A sharp, flat or natural written right before a note is an **accidental**. It applies to that note and to every later note on the same line or space **until the end of the bar** (the measure). The next bar starts fresh. You will meet bars properly in the rhythm lesson.

## Common mistakes

> **Counting octave numbers from A.** The number changes at C. The note just below C4 is B3, not B4.

> **Thinking every white key is a whole step from the next.** E–F and B–C are half steps. This single fact causes most early scale mistakes.

> **Reading bass clef as if it were treble.** The same position means a different note: the bottom line is E in treble clef but G in bass clef. Always check the clef first.

## Summary

Notes are named A to G, repeating every octave; octave numbers change at C, and middle C is C4. A half step is the smallest step (next key on the piano); a whole step is two. E–F and B–C are the only white-key half steps. Sharps raise by a half step, flats lower, naturals cancel. Treble clef lines are E G B D F and spaces F A C E; bass clef lines are G B D F A and spaces A C E G.

min: 50
```solution
An accidental is a sharp, flat or natural written in front of a note. It raises (sharp), lowers (flat) or restores (natural) that note by a half step, and it applies to that note and every later note on the same line or space until the end of the bar; the next bar starts fresh. C sharp and D flat are enharmonic equivalents: on a piano they are the same black key and sound the same, but they are spelled differently because a note's letter name depends on the scale or key it belongs to. In a key built on sharps, such as D major, the note is called C sharp; in a key built on flats it is called D flat.
```
