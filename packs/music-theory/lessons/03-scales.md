## Why scales

A **scale** is a set of notes in order of pitch that a piece of music mostly draws from. Knowing a piece's scale tells you which notes will sound "at home" and which will sound surprising. The **major scale** is the reference point for nearly everything else in Western theory: intervals, chords and keys are all described relative to it.

## The major scale formula

Play the white keys from C up to the next C: C D E F G A B C. That is the **C major scale**. Now look at the steps between neighbours, remembering that E–F and B–C are half steps:

| From → to | C→D | D→E | E→F | F→G | G→A | A→B | B→C |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Step | W | W | H | W | W | W | H |

**W W H W W W H** is the pattern of every major scale. Start on any note, follow the pattern, and you get that note's major scale. The half steps always fall between notes 3–4 and 7–8.

The notes of a scale are called **scale degrees** and are numbered 1 to 7 (8 is 1 again). Degree 1 is the **tonic**, the home note that gives the scale its name. Degree 5 is the **dominant** and degree 4 the **subdominant**; degree 7, a half step below the tonic, is the **leading tone** because it pulls strongly up to it.

## Building G major

Start on G and apply the pattern:

1. G → whole step → **A**
2. A → whole step → **B**
3. B → half step → **C**
4. C → whole step → **D**
5. D → whole step → **E**
6. E → whole step → **F♯** (E to F is only a half step, so we need F♯ to make a whole step)
7. F♯ → half step → **G**

G major is G A B C D E F♯ G: one sharp.

## The spelling rule

Every major scale uses **each letter exactly once**. That is why the seventh note of G major is F♯, not G♭: the scale already has a G, and it needs an F. The same rule decides between enharmonic names everywhere in this course.

## Key signatures

Writing the same sharps or flats before every note would be tiring, so music puts them once at the start of each staff, after the clef: the **key signature**. It applies to every note with that letter name, in every octave, for the whole piece (unless an accidental overrides it in a bar).

Sharps and flats in key signatures always appear in a fixed order:

- **Order of sharps:** F C G D A E B ("Father Charles Goes Down And Ends Battle")
- **Order of flats:** B E A D G C F (the same letters backwards; "BEAD Greatest Common Factor")

A key with three sharps has the first three: F♯, C♯, G♯. A key with two flats has B♭ and E♭.

### Which key is it?

- **Sharp keys:** the last sharp is the leading tone, so the key is a half step above it. Last sharp G♯ → key of A major.
- **Flat keys:** the second-to-last flat names the key. Flats B♭ E♭ A♭ → key of E♭ major. (F major, with only B♭, has to be memorised.)

| Key | Sharps or flats |
|---|---|
| C | none |
| G, D, A, E, B | 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 sharps |
| F, B♭, E♭, A♭ | 1, 2, 3, 4 flats |

## Natural minor in one paragraph

Every major key shares its key signature with a **relative minor**, whose tonic is degree 6 of the major scale. A minor uses the notes of C major starting from A: A B C D E F G A, pattern **W H W W H W W**. You will meet minor chords in lesson 5; the full story of minor keys (harmonic and melodic minor) is a stretch topic.

## Common mistakes

> **Spelling with a repeated letter.** F major is F G A B♭ C D E F, not F G A A♯ C D E F. Each letter once.

> **Getting the order of sharps wrong.** It is always F C G D A E B. A key with two sharps has F♯ and C♯, never F♯ and G♯.

> **Forgetting the key signature applies to every octave.** An F♯ in the signature makes every F sharp, high or low.

## Summary

Major scale pattern: W W H W W W H, with half steps between degrees 3–4 and 7–8. Each letter appears once. Degree 1 is the tonic, 4 the subdominant, 5 the dominant, 7 the leading tone. Order of sharps F C G D A E B; flats B E A D G C F. Sharp key = half step above the last sharp; flat key = second-to-last flat. The relative minor starts on degree 6.
